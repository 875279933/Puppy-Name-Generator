Are Dachshunds Aggressive Dogs?

An honest, research-based look at dachshund temperament for American owners

The Short Answer

Dachshunds are not aggressive by nature, but they are one of the more likely small breeds to growl, snap, or guard resources when they feel pushed. A 2008 study from the University of Pennsylvania, which surveyed owners of more than 30 dog breeds, ranked dachshunds near the top for aggression toward strangers and other dogs. That sounds alarming, but the same study showed that the behavior is usually tied to fear, lack of socialization, and a strong guarding instinct, not to a mean personality.

Where the Aggression Reputation Comes From

Dachshunds were originally bred in Germany to dig into badger dens. That job required a small, fearless dog that would not back down, even from an animal bigger than itself. The same independence that helped them in the field shows up at home as stubbornness and a willingness to defend their space.

Strong Prey Drive

Dachshunds were designed to chase small animals into burrows. That prey drive is still strong today, and it can look like aggression when a dog lunges at a squirrel, a cat, or a neighbor's toy poodle on a walk. It is not a character flaw. It is a working trait that has not faded in over a century.

Protective Instinct

These dogs bond tightly with their owners. They will bark at the mail carrier, growl at a stranger reaching for their food bowl, and snap at another dog that gets too close to their favorite person. It comes from the same place as loyalty, but without training it can cross the line.

What the Research Actually Shows

The most cited study on this topic is the University of Pennsylvania's 2008 breed aggression survey. Some key takeaways for dachshund owners:

  • Dachshunds scored higher than average for aggression toward strangers.
  • They were among the breeds more likely to guard food or toys.
  • Aggression toward family members was low to moderate, depending on training.
  • Smaller body size did not mean smaller risk. A bite from a 12-pound dog still hurts.

More recent U.S. data from veterinary behaviorists has not changed the picture much. Dachshunds are still on the watch list for resource guarding and stranger-directed aggression. The good news is that training makes a measurable difference. Dogs with structured early socialization drop several points down the aggression scale.

Types of Aggression Dachshunds Show

It helps to break the behavior into specific kinds, because each has a different fix.

Fear-Based Aggression

The most common type. A dachshund that was not exposed to different people, sounds, and environments as a puppy is more likely to react with barking or snapping when something new shows up. The dog is not mean. It is scared.

Resource Guarding

Dachshunds can become possessive over food, toys, beds, or even a favorite human. Growling when someone walks near the food bowl is a classic early sign. American trainers call this "RG" and treat it with trade-up games and structured "leave it" work.

Leash Reactivity

Many dachshunds lose their minds on the leash. They bark, lunge, and spin at other dogs. This is usually frustration or fear, not true aggression, but it gets the dog labeled "aggressive" at the dog park. The fix is distance, desensitization, and not forcing greetings.

Pain-Related Snapping

Because of their long backs, dachshunds are prone to back and joint pain. A dog in pain is more likely to snap when touched in a sore spot. This is one reason vets in the U.S. check for IVDD and arthritis before assuming a dog is "just aggressive."

How to Raise a Friendly Dachshund

Most aggression issues are preventable. U.S. trainers and behaviorists lean on the same core steps.

Socialize Early

The window is small. Between 3 and 14 weeks, a dachshund puppy should meet as many people, dogs, sounds, and surfaces as possible. Carry the puppy into a coffee shop, walk on different floors, and let strangers hand out treats. The goal is a dog that has seen the world before fear has a chance to set in.

Use Positive Reinforcement

Dachshunds are smart but sensitive. Yelling or physical punishment backfires fast and can actually make aggression worse. Reward-based training with small treats, praise, and play is the standard advice from American dog trainers.

Set Clear Rules

These dogs do best with structure. A dachshund that knows what is expected and where it stands in the family is calmer and more confident. Mixed signals, like allowing the dog on the couch some days and not others, lead to anxiety-driven behavior.

Manage the First Eight Weeks

Choose a breeder or rescue that starts socialization early. Puppies raised in busy homes with kids, other dogs, and a range of visitors tend to be easier to train. Neuter or Spay at the Right Time

U.S. veterinary behavior research suggests that spaying and neutering can reduce some forms of aggression, especially in males. Talk to your vet about timing. For most dachshunds, the sweet spot is between 6 and 12 months.

What a Well-Adjusted Dachshund Looks Like

The vast majority of dachshunds in American homes are friendly, affectionate, and a little cheeky. They greet their owners at the door, sleep under the bed covers, and play gently with kids they have known since puppyhood. They will bark at a stranger at the door. That is part of the job. The difference between a good dachshund and a problematic one almost always comes down to early socialization and clear rules.

When to Get Professional Help

If your dachshund growls, snaps, or bites, do not wait it out. A certified applied animal behaviorist (CAAB) or a veterinary behaviorist (DACVB) can rule out medical causes and build a training plan. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior has a referral list on its website. Most aggression cases improve a lot in 8 to 12 weeks of focused work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are dachshunds aggressive toward other dogs?

They can be, especially with dogs they do not know. Early socialization with calm, vaccinated dogs is the best prevention. Many dachshunds live happily with another small dog in the home but still bark at strange dogs on walks.

Are dachshunds aggressive toward children?

They are not aggressive by default, but they do not tolerate rough handling. Quick movements, ear pulling, or being picked up suddenly can cause a snap. American families with young kids are usually advised to supervise play and teach kids to leave the dog alone when it is eating or sleeping.

Are male or female dachshunds more aggressive?

Intact males are more likely to show dog-to-dog aggression, especially around other intact males. Females can be territorial, particularly with other females. Spaying and neutering at the recommended age usually reduces both.

Do dachshunds ever calm down?

Most dachshunds start to mellow around age 3 or 4. The high-energy puppy stage passes. With training, even a feisty adult can become the calm, steady companion the breed is known for.

Final Thoughts

Are dachshunds aggressive dogs? Not really. They are bold, protective, and a little stubborn, which gets misread as aggression. With early socialization, clear rules, and a kind but consistent owner, they turn into the loving, slightly clownish family dogs that show up in photos all over Instagram.
