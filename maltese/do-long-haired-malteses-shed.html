Do Long Haired Malteses Shed?

Honest shedding facts and grooming tips for American owners

The Short Answer

Long haired malteses do shed, but very little. The breed has hair instead of fur, and the hair grows in long, continuous cycles rather than dropping out in large seasonal coats. Most American owners notice only a few strands on a brush or in the comb after a grooming session. That is why malteses are often called a low-shed breed, but calling them "non-shed" is not quite right. Loose hair still ends up on the floor, on your clothes, and on the couch — just in small amounts compared to a Labrador or a beagle.

Hair vs Fur: Why It Matters

The difference between hair and fur is one of the most useful things to understand before answering do long haired malteses shed. Both are made of keratin and grow from follicles in the skin. The real difference is the growth cycle.

  • Fur (most dogs): Shorter growth cycle. Old hairs fall out in clumps, often in spring and fall. That is the seasonal coat blow you see with huskies, labs, and golden retrievers.
  • Hair (maltese and other low-shed breeds): Longer growth cycle. Hairs stay in the follicle longer, then drop one at a time. Owners see less tumbleweed-style shedding and more hair on the brush.

That is why a long haired maltese in full coat can look like a silk curtain, while a fur-coated dog of the same size looks short and dense. The hair keeps growing until it is cut or breaks.

How Much Shedding Is Normal

Expect to see:

  • Daily: A few strands on the brush, in the comb, or wrapped around the fingers after a petting session.
  • Weekly: A small puff of hair in the bathtub after a bath, plus a bit on the bathroom floor.
  • Yearly: No real seasonal coat blow. Maltese do not drop a winter coat in spring the way many breeds do.

If you are seeing clumps, bald patches, or sudden heavy shedding, that is not normal and a vet visit is worth scheduling. Hair loss in patches can point to allergies, skin infections, or hormonal issues.

Are Malteses Hypoallergenic?

No dog is fully hypoallergenic. People who react to dogs are usually reacting to a protein in the saliva, dander, and urine, not the hair itself. That said, malteses tend to cause fewer problems for mild allergy sufferers because:

  • Less hair falls onto the floor and into the air.
  • Regular bathing and brushing keeps dander levels down.
  • Smaller dogs produce less saliva and dander overall.

American families with mild allergies often do well with a maltese, but anyone with severe dog allergies should spend time with the breed before committing.

Grooming Routine for a Long Haired Maltese

A solid grooming routine keeps shedding under control and protects the coat from matting. Most US owners fall into a weekly rhythm.

Brushing

Brush three to four times a week with a pin brush and a metal comb. Always brush down to the skin so loose hair is removed from the undercoat area. Skipping a week can lead to tangles, and tangles pull out hair in clumps during the next brushing session.

Bathing

A bath every two to four weeks keeps the coat clean and the skin healthy. Use a mild, dog-specific shampoo, and follow with a conditioner made for long, white coats. Rinse thoroughly. Leftover soap leads to dry, itchy skin, which can lead to more scratching and more loose hair around the house.

Trimming

Many American owners keep a "puppy cut" that trims the coat to one to two inches all over. It is easier to maintain, looks tidy, and reduces the amount of hair that ends up around the house. Show owners keep the long coat, but that level of care is a full-time job.

Eyes, Ears, and Teeth

Tear staining around the eyes is common in the breed. Wipe the area daily with a soft, damp cloth. Check the ears weekly for wax and odor, and brush the teeth two to three times a week. Healthy skin, ears, and teeth all play a role in keeping the coat strong.

Shedding Compared to Other Small Breeds

Side by side with other US household favorites, the maltese sits on the lower end of the shedding scale:

  • Maltese: Very low
  • Yorkshire Terrier: Very low
  • Bichon Frise: Low
  • Poodle (Toy or Miniature): Very low
  • Pomeranian: Moderate (more seasonal blow)
  • Shih Tzu: Low to moderate

For an owner who wants a tiny, friendly companion and a clean couch, a maltese is one of the better choices on this list.

Tips to Keep Shedding Even Lower

A few small habits make a noticeable difference in how much hair ends up around the house:

  • Add a fish oil supplement to the daily food. Most US vets approve of omega-3 for coat health.
  • Run a HEPA air purifier in the room where the dog sleeps.
  • Wash dog beds and blankets once a week.
  • Vacuum with a pet-hair attachment two to three times a week.
  • Stick to a steady grooming schedule. Skipping sessions leads to matting and extra hair in the drain.

When Shedding Is a Warning Sign

Healthy maltese shedding looks like a few strands here and there. Hair loss that is patchy, sudden, or paired with red skin, scratching, or a bad smell is a reason to call the vet. Common culprits include food allergies, flea bites, mange, ringworm, and hypothyroidism. Catching these early saves the coat and the dog's comfort.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do long haired malteses shed more than short cut malteses? No. The amount of shedding is about the same. The difference is that long hair is more visible when it falls out.

Do malteses shed at all? Yes, but the amount is very small compared to fur-coated breeds.

Is a maltese a good choice for someone with allergies? Often, yes, but it depends on the person. Spend time with the breed before deciding.

Final Word

The honest answer to do long haired malteses shed is yes, but only a little. The breed's hair keeps growing and only drops in small amounts, which is why so many American owners with allergies or clean homes are happy with the choice. A steady grooming routine is the real work, and once it is in place, shedding is barely a blip on the household chore list.
